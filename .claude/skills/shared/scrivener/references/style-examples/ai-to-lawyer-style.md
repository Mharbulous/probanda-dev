# De-AI-ing a Draft: Converting AI Style to This Practice's Voice

AI-generated drafts are consistently identifiable, and clients notice. Before presenting any draft, strip the patterns below. Every example is a before/after pair from one email (fee arrears / stay application): the AI draft versus the lawyer's own final rewrite. The names, figures and matter are invented; the drafting moves are the real ones. The full pair is in `client-correspondence/2026-08-27-arrears-stay-email.md`.

## The tells, and the fixes

### 1. Self-narration

AI drafts announce their own structure: "First, the housekeeping, so the rest of this email makes sense", "Now the more important part", "So here's where things stand", "Here's what I will do". A lawyer states things; he doesn't preview that he's about to state them. Delete the signpost and let the paragraph order do the work — with one narrow exception: a short framing clause that changes the legal effect of what follows (e.g., "unless we are able to connect and you instruct otherwise") earns its place because it is a real qualifier, not a transition.

### 2. Emotional selling and asides

**AI:**
> Now the more important part. In my opinion, this application is beatable — Ian has already lost one stay application outright, and I expect this one to fail too, if it's properly answered. I want to fight it, and I want to make it as easy as possible for you to say yes.

**Lawyer:**
> In my opinion, the stay application should fail, but only if it is properly answered.

One plain sentence. No "I want to fight it with you", no em-dash aside restating history the client already knows, no selling. The opinion carries its own weight. Note where it sits in the final version: after the client has already been given the "if you can't pay" escape valve — the opinion reads as even-handed analysis offered once the client has an out, not as a pitch.

### 3. The "isn't X — it's Y" construction

**AI:**
> The August 28 deadline isn't arbitrary — it's the court's timeline, not mine.

**Lawyer:** (deleted; the reason appears as a concrete consequence instead)
> If payment arrives August 31 or September 2, I will be left with insufficient time to prepare a proper response, and the discount would no longer serve its purpose.

Justify a deadline with what happens if it is missed, not with rhetoric about the deadline itself. "Isn't X — it's Y" is one of the strongest AI tells; so are em-dash pivots and triads ("X, Y, and Z") used for rhythm rather than content.

### 4. Re-tabulating what a prior document already says

The AI draft recapped a prior offer as a four-bullet list (balance / discount / condition / allocation). The lawyer folded it into plain sentences, each with its own purpose stated inline:

> This is why my August 14 email offered a conditional $9,000 discount, to make answering more affordable. That is also why the offer is only valid if payment is received by 4:00 pm August 28, 2026.

Two short sentences, not one semicolon-joined compound — see item 5. Bullets are for genuinely tabular information (a hearing schedule with dates). Terms already set out in a prior letter get a one-line reference, not a restatement — the prior document governs anyway.

### 5. Semicolons doing a comma's job

**Draft in progress:**
> …to make answering more affordable; which is also why the offer is only valid…

**Final:**
> …to make answering more affordable. That is also why the offer is only valid…

A semicolon joins two independent clauses; it cannot precede "which." When a clause starting "which" or "that" explains the *reason* for the previous sentence rather than modifying a noun in it, end the first sentence and start a new one with "That is also why…" — this reads as spoken cadence, not as a grammar patch.

### 6. Framing advice around the lawyer's feelings instead of the client's situation, and leading with the client's actual problem

**AI:**
> A stay is not the outcome I want, but it protects your position: it gives you time to arrange payment...

**Lawyer, final order — the money problem comes first, the legal preference second:**
> If you currently lack the funds to pay your legal fees, consider consenting to the stay of arbitration that they are requesting, because a stay would be better than missing the September 11 arbitration deadline due to running out of funds for legal expenses. However, I would prefer answering the application properly to prevent a stay.

Address the client's actual situation directly ("If you currently lack the funds"), name the blunt downside, and state the recommendation as a preference with a reason — not as what the lawyer wants emotionally. Note the sequencing choice: the "can't pay" branch comes *before* the "here's my opinion on the merits" paragraph, not after — a client who can't pay reads their own situation addressed first, before being sold on the fight.

### 7. Vague actor, vague action

**AI:**
> If it hasn't, then unless you instruct me otherwise, I will contact opposing counsel and consent to a stay of the arbitration.

**Lawyer:**
> Here's what I will do unless we are able to connect and you instruct otherwise: At 4:00 pm tomorrow, I will check whether payment from you has been received. If it has, I will start working on answering the application immediately. If payment is still not received by Monday afternoon, then I will phone opposing counsel at around 2:00 pm and consent to a stay of arbitration.

Concrete specifics — *4:00 pm tomorrow*, *phone*, *Monday afternoon around 2:00 pm* — make a boundary credible. The instructions safeguard ("unless you instruct otherwise") is fronted once, plainly, rather than buried mid-sentence in parentheses as a hedge.

### 8. Softening a boundary the email exists to set

**AI:**
> If the timing of payment is the problem, call me today. That is easier to solve before the deadline than after.

**Lawyer:** (deleted entirely)

An exit-ramp invitation reopens the negotiation the email is closing. If the email's purpose is a firm boundary, end on the consequence. Do not append warmth to an ultimatum.

### 9. Trailing qualifiers that restate the obvious

**Draft in progress:**
> …consent to a stay of arbitration that they seek.

**Final:**
> …consent to a stay of arbitration.

Once "the stay application" has been established earlier in the email, "that they seek" / "that they are requesting" is only needed the first time it's referenced, not every time. Repeat the noun, not the clause.

## Quick checklist before presenting a draft

- No structural narration ("First...", "Now...", "So here's where things stand") — except a real legal qualifier ("unless you instruct otherwise")
- No "isn't X — it's Y"; minimal em-dashes; no decorative triads
- No selling or feelings ("I want to...", "fighting chance", "make it easy to say yes")
- Bullets only for tabular facts; prior-document terms referenced in one or two plain sentences, not re-tabulated
- No semicolon before "which"/"that" explaining a reason — split into two sentences instead
- Client's own problem (e.g., can't pay) addressed before the lawyer's legal opinion/preference
- Advice framed to the client's situation, with the blunt downside named
- Boundaries carry concrete specifics (who, when, how); safeguard language stated once, plainly, not buried in parentheses
- No exit ramps that reopen a boundary the email exists to close
- Don't repeat a qualifying clause once its noun is already established
- Short paragraphs, one idea each; plain words repeated rather than varied
