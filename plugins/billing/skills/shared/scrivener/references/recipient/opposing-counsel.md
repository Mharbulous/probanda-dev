# Wording a letter or email to opposing counsel

Read when: the recipient is the lawyer on the other side of a file. Every item in this file is the firm's own practice.

## Tone and Voice

The baseline is **collegial professionalism**. Opposing counsel is not the enemy: they are another officer of the court doing their job. Even when the relationship is adversarial, the correspondence should not be.

- **Firm, never hostile.** State your position clearly. Do not soften it with hedging, but do not add editorial commentary about opposing counsel's conduct unless you are building a record for a court application.
- **Counsel-to-counsel courtesy.** Use "you" and "your client", not first names for the opposing party.
- **No sarcasm, no passive aggression.** They read worse on paper, and undermine credibility in an affidavit.
- **Not every point deserves a reply.** Before answering a point from the other side's letter, classify it: points where silence would read as concession before a judge must be answered; jabs, editorializing and accusations peripheral to the letter's purpose are usually best met with a one-line acknowledgment-and-deferral ("We note your comments regarding X and will respond separately") or with nothing. Answering everything dilutes the two arguments that matter and surrenders the length advantage.

When opposing counsel is reasonable, be warm enough to preserve the working relationship. When they are not, be clinical. The shift in tone itself sends the message.

## Structure of a Typical Letter or Email

1. **Opening line.** State the file, the purpose, and any relevant deadline the request gives, up front: "We write regarding the outstanding document production under Rule 7-1." Do not waste the first paragraph on pleasantries or background the reader already knows.
2. **Body.** Organize by issue, with numbered paragraphs or subheadings for several topics. For each issue, state the relevant fact or procedural context (briefly), your position or request, and the consequence or next step if the request is not met.
3. **Closing.** End with a concrete next step and, where appropriate, the date the request gives: "Please confirm your client's position by April 25, 2026. If we do not hear from you by then, we will proceed with an application under Rule 7-1(10)."
4. **Sign-off.** "Yours truly" is standard in BC litigation correspondence. "Regards" is fine for email. Do not use "Respectfully" unless writing to the court: it reads as either submissive or sarcastic when directed at counsel.

## What to Include

- **File reference and context,** in a one-sentence refresher.
- **Your position, stated plainly.** Do not bury the point.
- **Deadlines.** Be specific: calendar dates, as the request gives them, not "within a reasonable time."
- **What you will do if you do not get a response,** as professional notice, not a threat.
- **References to the Rules or governing authority** when your position depends on them, as the request gives them.
- **An offer to discuss** where you genuinely want to resolve the issue without a court application.

## What to Avoid

- **Personal attacks or commentary on counsel's competence,** even if warranted.
- **Ultimatums you do not intend to follow through on.** If you say you will bring an application, bring it.
- **Over-explaining your legal position.** State enough to support your request; save the argument for court.

## When to Be Firm vs. Open to Discussion

**Be firm when** a deadline has passed and you have already been accommodating; the Rules clearly support your position and there is no reasonable basis for the other side's refusal; you need to build a paper trail for a court application; or compromise would prejudice your client.

**Be open when**, and only for positions not yet taken (once a position is on the record, Position Discipline below governs): scheduling or procedural matters where flexibility costs your client nothing; the other side has a defensible (even if wrong) position and resolving it by consent saves time and money; early-stage disputes where the relationship is still being established; or you want information or cooperation from the other side on a related issue.

Two tests, applied in order. First, the ratchet test: is this a new question, or a retreat from a position already taken? A retreat requires changed facts, not merely renewed pressure. Second, for genuinely new questions only, the judge test: if a judge would view refusal to discuss as unreasonable, be open; if the judge would view the other side's position as unreasonable, be firm.

## Position Discipline

Positions once taken (deadlines set, demands made, characterizations of events, consequences stated) are a floor, not an opening bid. Each successive letter is drafted against the full history, and the reader who matters (a judge, eventually) will have that history in front of them.

**The concession ratchet rule.** Never retreat from a previously taken position unless (a) the facts have actually changed since the position was taken, or (b) the practitioner explicitly instructs the retreat. "The other side pushed back" is not a change in facts. If a draft softens a deadline, waives a stated consequence, or re-characterizes an event more favourably to the other side than the last letter did, return the retreat as an open item and identify what changed: never let a concession leave the office unnoticed.

**Sympathetic is not the same as accommodating.** The writer should appear professional, knowledgeable, fair and sympathetic *to the judge*. A concession more generous than the likely court outcome gets **accepted**: the dispute never reaches a judge, and the sympathy the letter was written to earn is never seen. Concessions should always be worth less to the opponent than what a hearing would realistically give them.

**Repairing a prior inconsistent position.** When the position-consistency check reveals that a position the current draft wants to take is undercut by the firm's own earlier correspondence, do not deny the record and do not silently abandon the new position. Own it, then narrow it: acknowledge what the earlier letter said, then explain why events since, usually the other side's own conduct, have resolved the ambiguity in favour of the position now taken. A retreat the record explains is credibility preserved; a denial the record refutes is credibility spent.

## Handling Uncooperative or Bad-Faith Counsel

When opposing counsel is obstructive, unresponsive or acting in bad faith, the goal shifts from persuasion to documentation (`../audience/judge-as-exhibit.md`).

1. **Put everything in writing.** After a phone call, follow up with an email confirming what was discussed and agreed (or not agreed).
2. **Be specific about what you asked for and when,** so each request is traceable through the correspondence.
3. **State consequences clearly and follow through.** Hollow warnings undermine your credibility when you do bring the application.
4. **Do not match their tone.** Stay clinical. The contrast works in your favour when the correspondence is before the court.
5. **Escalate methodically.** Request, then reminder, then notice of intended application, then application. Courts expect reasonable efforts before granting relief.

## Specific Contexts

**Discovery disputes.** Cite the specific Rule (7-1 for document discovery, 7-2 for examinations) and the specific deficiency, as the request gives them. Quote or enclose the relevant demand so the record is self-contained. Propose a reasonable deadline for compliance, as the request gives it, before threatening an application. If seeking further and better production, identify the categories of documents you believe are missing and explain (briefly) why you believe they exist.

**Scheduling.** Propose multiple dates, from those the request gives: never ask "when are you available?" without offering options. Confirm agreements in writing the same day. When seeking adjournments, explain why (briefly), propose a new date, and offer a consent order.

**Demand letters.** State the factual basis for the claim concisely and the relief sought with specificity (amount, conduct, deadline). Set the response date the request gives (14 to 30 days is standard depending on complexity, and the practitioner sets it). Refer to your intention to commence proceedings if the demand is not met, but do not overstate the merits.

**Service of documents.** The covering letter identifies what is being served, the method of service, and the deemed date of service as the request gives it. List the documents by their identifying features, not their contents. Close with "Kindly acknowledge receipt in your usual way."

Settlement correspondence is written in the scrivener's without-prejudice mode.

## Example

A pattern to adapt, not fixed text.

### Example: Responding to an Aggressive Letter

> Dear Ms. Grant,
>
> We have your letter of April 10, 2026. We address your points in order.
>
> First, our client's list of documents was prepared in accordance with Rule 7-1(1) and is complete to the best of our knowledge as of the date it was served. Your suggestion that documents have been deliberately withheld is unfounded, and we trust it will not be repeated.
>
> Second, we have not agreed to produce the categories of documents described in paragraphs 4 through 7 of your letter. Those categories are overbroad and seek production that is neither relevant nor proportional to the matters in issue. We are prepared to discuss refined categories if you can identify the issues in the pleadings to which the requested documents relate.
>
> Third, the trial date was set by the court on consent. If your client wishes to seek an adjournment, that is a matter for an application. We are not in a position to consent.
>
> We are available to discuss any of the above by telephone if that would be productive.

It answers the unfounded allegation in one sentence, puts the burden of overbroad demands back on counsel, and offers to discuss without conceding anything. Point-by-point is right here only because each point would read as conceded if ignored: it is not the default.
