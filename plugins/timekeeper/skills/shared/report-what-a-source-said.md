# Reporting what a source said

Read when: a reply is about to tell the practitioner what a tribunal, court, arbitrator, registrar, opposing counsel, a party or a witness said or wrote, in an email, a letter, a direction, an order, a ruling, reasons, a transcript or an affidavit. This applies whether or not the practitioner asked to see the source.

A session read a tribunal's emails and reported that the tribunal had "reopened jurisdiction, in writing" and would "accept submissions on jurisdiction". Shown verbatim, the email only scheduled submissions on "jurisdictional issues" alongside the merits. Whether the respondent could still object was a separate question, decided later. The practitioner had to ask for the email before the overstatement came to light, and it touched their best argument. A characterization puts the session's reading between the practitioner and the words. The quote lets the practitioner read the words themselves.

## The rule

1. **Quote first.** For each source reported, quote its operative sentence or sentences verbatim, with the author, the date and the source: the mail message (sender and subject line) or the document (file name, and the page or paragraph where it has one). The quote comes before any summary of it.
2. **One quote per source.** When several sources are reported, quote each one under its own author and date. Never fold several messages into one summary.
3. **No word stronger than the source.** A summary of a direction, order or ruling uses the source's own verbs or weaker ones. Words such as "ordered", "ruled", "decided", "granted", "accepted", "agreed" or "reopened" appear only when the source says so. A schedule is not a ruling, a permission is not an order, and an invitation to make submissions on an issue does not decide the issue.
4. **Label the reading.** Anything beyond the quote, such as what the words mean, what they decided or what follows from them, starts with "My reading:" and sits apart from the quote. Where the source is silent on the point the practitioner cares about, say so: "The email does not say whether the objection is still open."
5. **Read before reporting.** Quote only from the source itself, read this session. A stored fact description, a summary written earlier, or another skill's report is someone's reading, not the source's words. Read the source at its cited place before reporting, or say that the report rests on that summary and name it.
6. **Law stays gated.** When a source's words are about the law, report them as what that source said. Whether the source is right about the law is a question for `/legal-research`. Never answer it through the wording of a characterization.

The quoted text is exempt from every brevity or conciseness instruction. Length spent quoting the operative words is never waste.

## Scope

This rule governs every report to the practitioner of what a source said: a chat reply, a briefing shown in chat, and a file written for the practitioner's own reading, such as a file-review memo, a briefing file or a handover, subject to `shared/deadline-isolation.md` where the file is written from a drafting session. It does not govern a Writing's substance or a reply draft, whose citation of a source is `/draft`'s, and it does not change how an approval ask is presented. When a report leads into an approval ask, `/show-me` governs the ask, and its rules apply unchanged: the source presented as a file, one decision, a plain-text question.

## Example (synthetic)

- **WRONG:** "The arbitrator reopened jurisdiction last month and said she would accept submissions on it."
- **RIGHT:** Arbitrator, email of 12 August, subject "Revised timetable": "The parties will deliver written submissions on the merits and on any jurisdictional issues by 30 September." My reading: this sets a date for submissions that may include jurisdictional issues. It does not say whether a jurisdictional objection is still open.
